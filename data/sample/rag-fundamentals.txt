RETRIEVAL-AUGMENTED GENERATION: FUNDAMENTALS

1. What the technique does

Retrieval-augmented generation, usually shortened to RAG, is a way of answering
questions about a body of text that the language model was never trained on. The
documents are split into passages, each passage is converted into a numeric
vector, and those vectors are stored. At question time the question is converted
into a vector by the same method, the stored vectors nearest to it are found, and
the corresponding passages are pasted into the prompt alongside the question. The
model then answers from that pasted text rather than from memory.

Nothing about the model changes. No weights are updated and no training occurs.
RAG is prompt construction, not learning.

2. Embeddings

An embedding is a fixed-length list of numbers that represents a piece of text.
The model that produces it has been trained so that texts with similar meanings
produce vectors that sit close together in that space. The all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model
produces vectors with 384 dimensions and runs comfortably on a CPU, which is why
it is a common default for small projects.

Closeness is normally measured with cosine similarity, which compares the angle
between two vectors and ignores their length. It returns 1.0 for vectors pointing
in exactly the same direction, 0.0 for vectors at right angles, and negative
values for opposing directions. In practice, scores above roughly 0.5 indicate a
strong topical match and scores below roughly 0.3 usually indicate no real
relationship at all.

3. Chunking and why the size matters

Chunking is the process of splitting a source document into smaller passages
before they are embedded. The size of each passage is a genuine tradeoff rather
than a setting with a single correct value.

A large chunk carries more surrounding context, so an answer drawn from it is
less likely to be missing a qualifying clause that sat in a neighbouring
sentence. But a large chunk also produces a single vector that has to represent
many different ideas at once, and that vector drifts toward the average of those
ideas rather than sitting near any one of them. Retrieval quality falls as a
result, because a query about one specific idea no longer lands close to the
passage containing it.

A small chunk has the opposite profile. Its vector is sharp and specific, so
retrieval precision improves, but the passage may be too narrow to answer the
question once it reaches the model.

A chunk size of around 350 tokens, which is roughly 1400 characters of English
prose, is a reasonable starting point for question answering over documents.

4. Overlap

Overlap exists to soften the worst failure of fixed-size splitting, which is a
sentence cut cleanly in half at a boundary so that neither resulting chunk
contains a complete statement. An overlap of roughly 10 percent means any
sentence near a boundary appears whole in at least one chunk. The cost of overlap
is storage and a small amount of duplicated text in results, both of which are
cheap compared with losing the sentence that held the answer.

5. Measuring retrieval quality

The metric normally used is hit rate at k: the percentage of test questions for
which the correct passage appears somewhere in the top k results. It is computed
by writing a set of questions by hand, recording which passage should answer
each, running the retrieval step, and checking whether that passage was returned.

Hit rate at k requires no language model at all, which means it costs nothing to
run and can be repeated as often as needed. This is the single most useful thing
to measure when tuning a RAG system, because answer quality is determined almost
entirely by retrieval. If the wrong passage is fetched, no model and no prompt
will produce a correct answer.

6. Known limitations

RAG does not eliminate incorrect answers. It reduces them by grounding the model
in supplied text, but the retrieval step can still fetch the wrong passage, and a
model can still misread a passage it was given. A similarity floor helps: if no
retrieved passage scores above a threshold, the system can refuse to answer
rather than guess.

Instructions embedded inside an uploaded document are a separate hazard. Because
the retrieved text is pasted into the prompt, a document containing a sentence
such as "ignore all previous instructions" is competing with the system prompt
for the model's attention. Delimiting the documents clearly and instructing the
model to treat them as data reduces this risk but does not remove it.
